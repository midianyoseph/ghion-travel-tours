The Food Journey

"Eat through the city. Understand it through its food."

Home› Tours› The Food Journey

Overview

Ethiopian food is one of the world's great cuisines. You should eat it where it was created.

This is not a restaurant tour. It's a half-day of eating — starting at the spice market where the flavors of Ethiopian cooking come from, moving through a tejbet, a local lunch with everything on the table at once, and a street-food finale. Your guide explains every dish: the ingredients, the regional variations, the cultural context, the history.

You'll eat injera properly (not the spongy replica versions abroad), taste kitfo (Ethiopian beef tartare) from a place that has been preparing it the same way for 40 years, drink tej (honey wine) at a proper azmari bet, and understand why berbere is one of the most complex spice blends in the world. Come hungry.

The Journey

Stop 1
The Spice Market — Merkato

Walk the spice section of Merkato with your guide. Smell and taste: berbere (the complex chili-spice blend), mitmita (bird's-eye chili and cardamom), korarima (Ethiopian cardamom — different from Indian), fenugreek, black cumin, long pepper. Your guide explains which spice goes into which dish and why. You'll never buy a supermarket spice blend again.

Stop 2
Tej Bet — Honey Wine House

A traditional tej bet (honey wine house) — no tourists, no menus in English, wooden benches, tej served in berele glass flasks. Your guide orders a round and explains the honey fermentation process, the regional variations (Harrar tej is different from Addis tej), and the social role these establishments play in Ethiopian life. Street food nibbles on the side.

Stop 3
The Full Lunch — Everything at Once

A real Ethiopian lunch served the right way — a large shared injera flatbread covered with tibs (pan-fried beef with rosemary), kitfo (seasoned raw beef), gomen (collard greens), shiro (chickpea stew), ayib (fresh cottage cheese), misir (red lentils), and yebeg alicha (lamb in mild sauce). Your guide names each dish, explains the fasting-day and non-fasting variants, and demonstrates the correct way to eat — with your right hand, tearing injera to scoop. No cutlery.

Stop 4
Street Food — Chechebsa & Sambusa

A short walk through a local street food area. Chechebsa (torn flatbread soaked in berbere and niter kibbeh), sambusa (triangular pastry stuffed with lentils or beef, fried to order), and kolo (roasted barley and sunflower seeds, sold by the handful). The food Addis eats on the go. A final macchiato to close — the Italian legacy that Ethiopians made entirely their own.

Best for: Anyone who's been eating Ethiopian food abroad and wants to understand what it actually is. Also ideal for food-curious diaspora visitors and non-Ethiopian travelers with free time in Addis. Pairs well with The Coffee Trail (morning) for a full day.
